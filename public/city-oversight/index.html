City Oversight

Police accountability also means holding city leaders accountable for their decisions involving the department. This page brings together records about how the city oversees, funds, and responds to issues involving the American Fork Police Department.

City Council Meetings

This section collects public comments and council-meeting material that meaningfully bears on AFPD accountability, policy, oversight, victim services, major operations, or significant resource decisions. Routine ceremonies, general praise, community events, and passing police references are omitted.

Context written for this site is clearly separated from passages drawn from the official minutes. The minutes are summaries: if a meeting contains police-related public comments but no Police Oversight and Governance section, no qualifying official response or discussion was found in the published minutes reviewed—not necessarily that no discussion occurred elsewhere.

      • Victim Advocacy Program operations and law-enforcement response

        • Context written for this site: The council reviewed victim-service demand, follow-up practices, reactions to arrests, community partnerships, referrals, reparations, and how advocates carry victims’ concerns into the prosecution process. Relevant passages from the official meeting minutes: Ms. Whiting presented the Victim Advocacy Program's quarterly statistics and provided an overview of services delivered during the reporting period. For the third quarter, covering January through March, the program served 218 victims, including both primary victims and secondary victims such as children who were present during incidents or otherwise affected by the events. Domestic violence continued to be the largest service category. Advocates provided 1,653 service contacts and assistance activities, including follow-up contacts, referrals, safety planning, resource coordination, and other advocacy services. Council Member Hunter asked for clarification regarding the statistics, noting that the report showed 218 victims were served but 342 victims contacted. Ms. Whiting explained that the 218 figure reflected individual victims who received services, while the 342 contacts included repeated follow-up, court notifications, welfare checks, resource coordination, and contact through a non-offending parent when children were involved. Mayor Frost noted that victims respond differently to law enforcement actions, with some feeling relieved and others struggling because of their relationship with the offender. Ms. Whiting agreed, explaining that advocates help victims understand their options, process events, and focus on safety and recovery. Council Member Carroll asked whether the program serves both American Fork and Cedar Hills, and Ms. Whiting confirmed that it does, with most cases originating in American Fork. Carroll also asked whether demand had increased as the city grew. Ms. Whiting said service levels were roughly 50 percent higher than six years earlier and that adding Ms. Nordin had helped the program keep pace. City Administrator Bunker asked about key community partners and referrals for victims needing more extensive assistance. The advocates identified The Refuge and the Children's Justice Center and explained that the City’s advocates provide crisis intervention, emotional support, education, and guidance through the criminal justice process while connecting victims to community partners for counseling, shelter, and ongoing support. Council Member Holley asked whether advocates help connect victims with therapists and other professional services. Ms. Whiting said the program provides referrals and resource lists but does not recommend specific providers. Council Member John asked about crime victim reparations funding. Ms. Nordin explained that eligible victims may receive financial assistance for crime-related medical care, counseling, relocation, and other expenses. She added that advocates meet weekly with the City’s prosecutor to discuss cases and communicate victims’ concerns and desired outcomes.       • Police facility planning and public-safety impact fees

        • Context written for this site: Consultants presented the assumptions and service-demand data supporting proposed public-safety impact fees, including a planned police facility and increasing police call volume. No council member is recorded making a substantive police-specific comment during this portion. Relevant passages from the official meeting minutes: Susan Becker of Zions Public Finance explained that the analysis assumed construction of a new police facility in approximately 2028 at an estimated cost of roughly $13 million. Public-safety impact fees were calculated from facility costs and the number of police and fire calls generated by different types of development. City staff categorized police and fire calls by land-use type. The analysis found that a typical single-family residence generated approximately 1.23 police calls per year. Ms. Becker noted that police call volumes had increased over time and that some calls involved alarms and other routine service requests. The methodology combined capital cost per service call with average demand to calculate the maximum supportable fee. The proposed police facility’s estimated cost was divided by its planned square footage to establish a cost-per-square-foot figure. Impact fees could fund qualifying capital assets but not ongoing expenses such as personnel. Although police and fire impacts were calculated separately, Utah law treated them as components of one public-safety impact fee. View minutes, page 20 · View minutes, page 21

Under American Fork City Code Chapter 5.04, the City Council may revoke a business license if the licensee engages in unlawful activity, violates applicable state laws, codes or standards of professional ethics, city ordinances, or permits criminal conduct on the premises. 2. Under American Fork City Code §5.44.050, it is unlawful for any employee, customer, or massage technician in a massage establishment to engage in prohibited activity on the premises. 3. Under American Fork City Code § 5.44.060 any violation of Chapter 5 of the American Fork City Code may result in the revocation of a business license. 4. Utah Code § 58-47b-501 et seq. the Massage Therapy Practice Act prohibits massage therapists or massage establishments from engaging in unlawful conduct. 5. Utah Admin. Code R156-47b-502 defines what constitutes unprofessional conduct by a massage therapist or massage establishment. Findings 1. Millett Massage received notice of this hearing as required by American Fork City Code § 5.04.230. 2. Millet Massage has appeared at the revocation hearing. 3. Millett Massage employees engaged in activity in violation of American Fork City Code §5.44.050. 4. Multiple customers corroborated they have received prohibited services at the premises. 5. Two employees of Millett Massage have been criminally charged based on conduct occurring at the licensed premises. 6. The two employees of Millett Massage have engaged in unprofessional conduct as defined in Utah Admin. Code and unlawful conduct as defined by Utah Code.
