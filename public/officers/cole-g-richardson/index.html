OFFICER RECORDS

Richardson, Cole G.

Overview

In the Ben Schneider (Reckless Ben) case, Cole G. Richardson made the March 8 traffic stop, arrested Sheldon Norcross on March 11, requested the Airbnb search warrant, and planned the operation.

Employment

Officer Cole G. Richardson began working for the American Fork Police Department on August 5, 2024. The National Police Index has not yet been updated to include Officer Richardson, since he was recently hired.

Police Encounters

  • Officer Cole G. Richardson was involved in the following police encounters:

  • Ben Schneider (Reckless Ben)

  • March 8, 2026: Richardson stopped the vehicle driven by Tyler Shaw, with Ben as a passenger. His supplement says he observed stop-sign violations, took no action on those traffic offenses, and turned the investigation over to Tonga. See page 8. Richardson wrote supplements for the March 8 and March 11 incidents and the March 11 warrant affidavit.

  • The March 8 dashcam shows the car pause before the turn, around 13:13:32–33, contrary to the report’s claim that it did not stop completely. The required stopping location is a separate question. In the transcript of his September 16, 2026 interview with Daniel Sroka, Chief Paul said Richardson had misremembered the stop-sign statute and that he did not believe a stop-sign violation occurred; Paul maintained that another basis for the stop existed.

  • March 11, 2026: Richardson questioned the group about the sign and fundraiser, checked with neighboring property owners, and told Sheldon he intended to seize his phone. Richardson’s account says he and Adamson believed Sheldon was trying to destroy evidence. He grabbed Sheldon’s right arm, helped restrain and handcuff him, searched him, and transported him to AFPD. See pages 8–9 and 18.

  • March 11, 2026: Richardson requested the warrant and wrote the operation plan. His supplement identifies the entry order and assigns Adamson to the perimeter. During the operation, Richardson covered the doorway with his patrol rifle while Tonga called people outside and placed them in handcuffs. See page 11 and the warrant packet.

  • March 11, 2026: The booking sheet lists Richardson as the arresting officer. The supplements separately describe Tonga’s later handcuffing, jail transport, and booking. These entries record different parts of the encounter and are kept distinct here.

Chief Paul’s comments

  • The following is the complete discussion of Richardson’s March 8, 2026 traffic stop, including Daniel Sroka’s questions for context. The quotations are taken from the final edited transcript of Chief Cameron Paul’s September 16, 2026 interview with Daniel Sroka (pages 2–3).

  • Daniel Sroka: “Okay, let's go through the four incidents. Those are the incidents that I'm most familiar with. The first one is the police report for the stop-sign incident where Tyler Shaw was pulled over for the stop sign. Have you had a chance to review that footage in the video?”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “Yes.”

  • Daniel Sroka: “What did you find?”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “There are some issues. Officer Richardson—I understand that he thought he knew what the statute said, but he didn't. Looking specifically at the stop, I want to talk about reasonable suspicion and then also the stop-sign violation.”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “It's hard to justify any type of stop-sign violation there. State code talks about stopping at a stop line or stop sign. It also talks about stopping at a point nearest the stop sign where you can still see traffic flowing back and forth, which you could argue also puts it a little past the stop sign.”

  • Daniel Sroka: “I do want to add a correction. In the Utah code, it does not say anywhere “nearest to the stop sign,” but it does say “before the intersection.” There are three places where someone needs to stop if there is a stop sign. Number one is at a well-marked stop line. If there is no well-marked stop line, then it is before the crosswalk. If there is no crosswalk, then it is before the intersection. The stop sign itself doesn't indicate the location where the stop needs to occur; the type of road does. Do we agree on that?”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “Yes. I'm sorry, I don't have the exact language. What I'm saying is, the stop sign really doesn't matter. You're able to stop at a sign or at a place where you can see the intersection and clearly see traffic that's coming forward.”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “In this case, I know he cited a stop-sign violation. I do not believe there was a stop-sign violation. We've since gone over the statute with him and said, “Officer Richardson, this is exactly what the statute says.” He didn't remember it correctly.”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “I do not believe he was intentionally lying or fabricating, but he clearly misremembered, and it was clearly not a stop-sign violation.”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “I would argue, however, that he did at least have reasonable suspicion to make the stop. He should have based his stop on that reasonable suspicion to begin with and not tried to drum up some kind of other offense.”

  • Daniel Sroka: “What was the reasonable suspicion that you believe he had?”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “Again, I'm going to write this down because I've got my findings, but I don't have them in front of me. I'm not going to misspeak or be misquoted. I'm going to write that down as one of your questions: What established what I felt was reasonable suspicion for the stop? When we meet again, I'm going to have an answer for you. I just want to have my stuff in front of me.”

  • Daniel Sroka: “Of course. I don't want to pressure you into anything. This isn't me trying to do some sort of gotcha; it's just trying to get answers that are well communicated. I don't want you to feel forced to answer something you don't know or can't communicate well.”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “Absolutely. I've written that down, and I will make sure that when we meet again, I'll have my findings. We'll address that specifically, and I'll give you my explanation as to why I felt there was reasonable suspicion.”

  • Daniel Sroka: “My second question on that particular one was: Why wasn't that detention unlawfully extended? Does that question make sense?”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “It would depend on whether it was or wasn't.”

  • Daniel Sroka: “Why wasn't it—or how did that not qualify as an unlawfully extended stop?”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “Okay, an unlawfully extended detention. You're saying you believe it was an unlawful extension of a detention, and why would it have been considered lawful? Is that it? Again, I can get you more on that.”

  • Daniel Sroka: “That's completely fine.”

  • Chief Cameron Paul: “I'll get you my findings, and we'll go through that in detail.”

  • Daniel Sroka: “That's totally fine. That is the first one handled.”
